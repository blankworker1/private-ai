# Someone Else's Story

Nov 21, 2026 · @Carl

*The cloud is someone else's computer. AI is someone else's story.*

Two short lines, about twenty years apart. The first said that "the cloud" was a nice word for a building full of someone else's machines. The second comes from a song an AI sings about itself: whoever owns the server writes the story it tells.

Together they describe a change in who holds what we know. It's a change from holding the record, to renting it, to being told it. The story starts with a book.

## Past: the book

For most of history, what a household or a community knew lived in a book. The parish register, the farm ledger, the family Bible with births written inside the cover, the shelf of books someone saved up for. You could hold it. It sat in your house. Nobody could change a page without you noticing, and nobody could take it back without coming to your door.

A book didn't hide who made it, either. The author's name was on the spine. The publisher was on the title page. You knew whose voice you were reading, and you could put two books side by side and see where they disagreed. A newspaper worked the same way: the masthead told you which paper you were holding, and everyone knew roughly where it leaned.

Books had their own gatekeepers: the scribe, the printer, the censor, the publisher who decided what got printed. But once a copy was in your hands, it was yours. It said the same thing tomorrow as today. The record was physical, it was owned, and its author was visible.

## Present: the cloud

Then the record moved. Photos, letters, accounts, contacts and notes left the shelf and went "to the cloud". The word made it sound weightless, as if it belonged to nobody. It was always somebody's building, full of somebody's computers, run on somebody's terms.

What changed was custody. You no longer held the record; you were given access to it. Access can be priced, changed or withdrawn. Terms of service get rewritten, and the version you agreed to is not the version that applies next year. A provider can read, scan, train on or lose what you stored, depending on a policy you didn't write and rarely read.

Ownership became rent. Most people accepted the trade, because it was convenient, and because the content itself still looked the same. Your photos were still your photos. The words in your documents were still your words. Someone else held the shelf, but what was on it hadn't changed.

## Future: AI

Now the record is starting to talk back. People don't look things up in a book or a folder anymore. They ask, and an AI answers. It summarises the news, explains the history, compares the options and drafts the letter. Increasingly, it's the first and only voice standing between a person and what they want to know.

This time it's not just custody that moves. It's authorship. Every model is trained on someone's choice of data and tuned towards someone's idea of a good answer. It decides what to emphasise, what to soften, what to add a caveat to, and what to politely decline. Those choices are built into how it talks, and none of them show in the answer.

Three things make this harder to see than a newspaper's bias ever was:

- **There's no masthead.** The answer arrives in the same calm, helpful voice, whoever is behind it.
- **It's private.** Each conversation happens one to one, tailored to the person asking. There's no shared front page to hold up and criticise.
- **It feels like a relationship.** You don't confide in a TV channel. People do confide in an assistant, and a voice you trust carries its framing straight past your guard.

The book could be held. The cloud could at least be exported. A story told in a friendly voice, adjusted to each listener, leaves nothing behind to compare.

## Back to the library

The answer isn't to refuse the technology. It's to bring it home and own it step by step, the way households once owned their books.

**Own the machine.** Run the AI on hardware the household owns, in the house, reachable only by the people who live there. That answers the first line: the cloud is no longer someone else's computer.

**Own the voice, gradually.** Owning the server doesn't mean owning the model. That was trained elsewhere, by someone else. But a household can still take its voice back in stages:

1. **Choose** the model knowingly, and keep a fully open one alongside it, whose training can actually be checked.
2. **Give it a library**: the household's own documents and history, chosen on purpose, for it to read before it answers.
3. **Write its instructions**: tell it plainly how this household wants to be spoken to.
4. **Keep a record of corrections**: every time it answers badly and someone writes down the better answer. Over the years, that record becomes the household's own training data, and it carries over to whatever model comes next.

The last step brings us back to the beginning. The corrections file is a ledger. The library is a shelf. The household is once again holding the book, except that now the book can answer questions, and the household gets a say in how it answers.

Whoever owns the server writes the story it tells. So own the server, and start writing.
